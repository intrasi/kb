# Release cadence, not clones, sells protection

*Synthesised 2026-11-08 from the research notes in `research_notes/Device cloning and build cadence/`, building on the machine-software cloning report (2026-11-08) and the vehicle-firmware anti-tampering report (2026-11-07) in `sources/`. Business and legal level only; no attack methods. Not legal or financial advice. All prices in the proposed model are our assumptions, not market data.*

The public record does not support the story that Chinese clone makers take the algorithms out of shipped Samsung, Dyson, iRobot, DJI or Xiaomi devices and sell cheaper copies, so "stop the cloners" is not a documented, costly problem we can sell against today. The big, court-proven losses from copied device software all came from **insiders who carried source code or process data to a rival**: Motorola v. Hytera ($407.4M award affirmed), ASML v. XTAL ($845M, uncollectable), Samsung's DRAM leaks to CXMT (trillions of won estimated), and Sinovel (>$550M). Protecting the shipped binary would have stopped none of them. Fights between consumer-device makers are **patent and design cases** (iRobot v. SharkNinja, Dyson v. Dreame and Laifen, DJI v. Autel, Ecovacs v. Roborock), and defendants often win by invalidating the patent. Copying of the shipped product itself is documented mainly for **consumables and accessories** (clone ink cartridges, chargers) and in **one firmware case**, Ubiquiti v. Cambium, which settled for an undisclosed sum. The stronger finding is about cadence. Devices keep shipping release builds for years: about 4 to 5 a year for a DJI drone, a 7-model Roborock firmware wave in one week, and **165 Tesla software versions in 12 months**. The CRA, the EU phone ecodesign rule and UN R156 make that stream mandatory and long. A per-build price can work, but only as a measuring unit inside an annual commitment: count release builds, never CI builds, discount security-only releases, and cap the bill. Pure pay-per-build has no precedent in app protection, and buyers have pushed back hard against unpredictable usage bills. **Verdict: lead with anti-tampering and release-cadence compliance, use cloning only as a secondary "weak patents" argument, and test the pricing with three buyers before building billing.**

## Costly copying runs through people and patents, not teardowns

The largest documented cases all begin with a person leaving with source code or process data. A jury awarded Motorola $764.6M against Hytera after former Motorola engineers took DMR radio source code that ended up in Hytera products. The court cut this to **$407.4M**, and the Seventh Circuit affirmed that amount in July 2024 ([7th Cir. opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca7/22-2370/22-2370-2024-07-02.html)). In 2025 a court ordered further royalties of more than $59M because Hytera kept using the secrets ([Motorola Solutions](https://www.motorolasolutions.com/newsroom/press-releases/hytera-continues-to-use-stolen-trade-secrets.html)). ASML won **$845M** against XTAL, founded by former ASML engineers, one of whom downloaded about 2 million lines of source code. XTAL was bankrupt, so ASML took its IP instead of cash ([ASML 6-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/937966/000119312519163369/d713687dex991.htm)). Korean prosecutors put one copycat-fab case at a loss of at least 300B won ([Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/law-crime/20230612/ex-samsung-exec-indicted-for-stealing-trade-secrets-to-build-copycat-plant-in-china)). A former Samsung engineer who leaked DRAM process technology to CXMT got 7 years in April 2026, cut to 5 on appeal ([Seoul Economic Daily](https://en.sedaily.com/society/2026/10/22/ex-samsung-engineer-gets-5-years-for-leaking-chip-tech-to)). The Apple self-driving engineer who took data before joining Xpeng followed the same pattern ([DOJ](https://justice.gov/usao-ndca/pr/former-apple-employee-indicted-theft-trade-secrets)). Korea's own statistics frame the problem the same way: **107 overseas technology leaks in 2021-2025, about 20 a year**, mostly chips and displays, almost all through people ([Aju Press](https://www.ajupress.com/view/20260927105829655)). These are fab and process secrets. They are never shipped inside a device, so binary protection is irrelevant to them.

The rivalries the brief names are patent and design fights, and the incumbent often loses. iRobot won an ITC import ban on one Shark product in 2023, which Shark had already redesigned, and its Massachusetts suit ended after IPR proceedings weakened its patents ([iRobot IR](https://investor.irobot.com/news-releases/news-release-details/irobot-announces-favorable-initial-determination-patent-0); [Patsnap](https://www.patsnap.com/resources/blog/litigation/irobot-vs-sharkninja-how-ipr-proceedings-dismantled-a-robot-vacuum-patent-suit-patsnap-eureka/)). China's patent office invalidated Dyson's main Chinese hair-dryer patent after a Dreame challenge, and Laifen invalidated a Dyson design patent in 2025. Dyson's cash wins are small, such as a >$256K default judgment against online sellers ([China IP Magazine](https://www.chinaipmagazine.com/en/news-show.asp?id=12037); [Patsnap](https://www.patsnap.com/resources/blog/litigation/dyson-wins-default-judgment-in-design-patent-case-against-40-e-commerce-sellers-patsnap-eureka/)). No Dyson case alleging copied motor-control software was found. DJI v. Autel mixed a design claim with a hired-engineer claim and settled days before trial ([DroneDJ](https://dronedj.com/2021/08/19/dji-autel-settle-years-long-patent-dispute-days-before-jury-trial/)). Ecovacs v. Roborock is a Chinese-versus-Chinese patent war in European courts ([UPC Court of Appeal](https://www.unifiedpatentcourt.org/sites/default/files/files/api_order/Final%20order%203-2026%20signed-1.pdf)). The largest consumer verdict, **$634.3M** for Masimo against Apple, runs in the opposite direction: a small firm's patent against a giant ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/11/15/jury-says-apple-owes-masimo-634m-for-patent-infringement/)).

There is a legal reason why shipped-product copying rarely shows up in court. Studying a product that was lawfully bought is a lawful way to acquire a trade secret in both the EU ([Directive 2016/943, Art. 3](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2016/943/oj)) and the US, where the DTSA excludes reverse engineering from "improper means" ([18 U.S.C. 1839](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1839)). A maker whose algorithm is worked out from a sold device often has no trade-secret claim, only patent or copyright claims. The case may happen and still never be litigated as "copying." So the absence of cases is weak evidence that the problem is absent. It is strong evidence that the problem **cannot be proven or priced** from public data, and that is what matters for a sales pitch.

| Route | Documented examples | Size of documented loss | Does protecting shipped code help? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Insider theft of source or process data | Hytera, XTAL, Samsung/CXMT, Sinovel, Apple/Xpeng | Hundreds of millions to trillions of won | **No.** Needs access control, DLP, HR and legal measures |
| Patents and designs | iRobot/Shark, Dyson/Dreame/Laifen, DJI/Autel, Ecovacs/Roborock, Masimo/Apple | Large verdicts are rare; many patents invalidated | Indirectly at best: protects know-how that weak patents fail to cover |
| Consumables and accessories | Epson, HP and Canon ITC exclusion orders; CBP seizures of fake Apple chargers | Measured as trademark seizures, not software loss | Partly, but OEMs already use authentication chips, and backlash is high |
| Analysis of the shipped product | Ubiquiti v. Cambium (rival firmware on Ubiquiti hardware) | Undisclosed settlement | **Yes**, the only route where it directly applies |

## Where shipped-code copying is real, buyers already pay or face backlash

The consumables trade is real and large, and OEMs already spend money fighting it. In August 2026 the USITC issued Epson a **general exclusion order** against clone ink cartridges, and it had earlier fined Ninestar **$11.1M** for breaching an older order ([The Recycler](https://therecycler.com/posts/usitc-issues-epson-ink-cartridge-exclusion-order/)). In May 2025 CBP Houston seized **373,000 counterfeit Apple chargers and cables** worth about $7M at retail ([CBP](https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/local-media-release/cbp-houston-announces-seizure-counterfeit-apple-phone-chargers-worth)). Global counterfeit trade reached **$467B, 2.3% of world imports**, and electronics is among the most targeted categories ([OECD/EUIPO 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2025/05/global-trade-in-fake-goods-reached-USD-467-billion-posing-risks-to-consumer-safety-and-compromising-intellectual-property.html)). These figures count trademark fakes, not copied code. The defence in this segment is accessory authentication, and it carries a warning. HP's January 2026 firmware that disables non-HP cartridges drew a formal complaint from the trade group I-ITC under EPEAT ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/hp-ink-blocking-firmware-may-163527568.html)). A protection vendor that helps lock out third-party parts inherits right-to-repair backlash, the same risk the earlier machine-software report found in the Newag train case.

Ubiquiti v. Cambium is the single clean example of a rival profiting from a maker's shipped product. Cambium sold firmware to replace Ubiquiti's own on Ubiquiti radios, to take over the installed base. Ubiquiti sued under computer-crime and DMCA claims. The court required Ubiquiti to separate its own code from the open-source parts, and the case settled in December 2020 with an undisclosed payment ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/illinois/ilndce/1:2018cv05369/355208/59/); [Cambium](https://www.cambiumnetworks.com/blog/cambium-networks-corporation-announcement-of-litigation-settlement/)). That gives three honest buyer profiles for anti-copying, ranked by evidence: OEMs with consumable ecosystems (strong evidence, high backlash), makers whose installed base a rival can take over with its own firmware (one case), and makers of valuable on-device algorithms whose patents keep failing, such as robot-vacuum navigation (plausible motive, no proven extraction). The third is the most natural fit for us, and it is the least proven. Emproof also already sells to it, with a public claim of "formal proofs such as translation validation" for embedded obfuscation ([Emproof](https://emproof.com/en/solution)).

## Devices ship 4 to 165 release builds a year, and the law keeps them coming

Release cadence is better documented than cloning, and it is the fact that matters for pricing. **Flagship phones now get 7 years of OS and security updates**: Pixel 8 and later, and Samsung from the Galaxy S24 ([Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/phone-update-policies-1658633/); [TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-samsung-vs-google-android-software-support/)). Samsung puts devices on monthly, quarterly or twice-yearly security schedules. A monthly-tier flagship therefore gets roughly 12 to 16 public builds a year and 85 to 110 over its life, before carrier and region variants. That is our estimate from the tier definitions, not a count. We found no Xiaomi HyperOS cadence data. Cars span two orders of magnitude. Tessie's fleet tracker recorded **165 distinct Tesla versions in the last 12 months** and 1,221 in total ([Tessie](https://stats.tessie.com/)), grouped in 2025 into about 9 major branches ([TeslaFi](https://teslafi.com/firmware)). XPeng called its August 2025 release "the third major OTA release of 2025" ([XPeng](https://www.xpeng.com.my/news/2025-08-25-OTA-Press-Release-1.pdf)). In 2022, Mercedes and VW shipped OTA updates about every 12 weeks ([Electrek](https://electrek.co/2022/06/07/over-the-air-updates-how-does-each-ev-automaker-compare/)).

Consumer devices sit at the low end per model and grow with the number of models. DJI's Mini 4 Pro had **about 4 to 5 aircraft firmware releases in 2024** and about 10 in two years, each with matching remote-controller firmware ([DJI release notes](https://dl.djicdn.com/downloads/DJI_Mini_4_Pro/20250513/DJI_Mini_4_Pro_Release_Notes_EN.pdf)). Roborock pushed one Matter update to **7 vacuum models in 10 days**, each with its own build number ([Roborock](https://newsroom.roborock.com/gl/news/roborock-releases-matter-protocol-update-leading-smart-home-integration)). Bosch eBike shipped several drive-system feature updates in 2025-26, including a 100 Nm boost and Battery Lock ([Bosch eBike](https://www.bosch-ebike.com/us/service/software-updates)). We found no per-year counts for iRobot, Ecovacs, Dyson, Shimano or Xiaomi appliances. For pricing, two facts matter more than any single rate. First, one car release bundles many ECU images. Second, a firmware wave produces one build per model. Billable volume therefore grows with model breadth as much as with release frequency.

Internal builds dwarf releases. The only number found is a consultant's 2022 figure for Tesla: **more than 20 vehicle software builds a day against about 150 customer releases a year** ([Rohde, IEEE slides](https://r6.ieee.org/scv-pses/wp-content/uploads/sites/17/2022/03/Florian-slides-March-2022-presentation.pdf)). Billable release builds would be about 2% of CI volume. Billing CI builds would put protection in every developer's inner loop and invite rationing. Billing only release builds keeps the unit small, forecastable and tied to what customers actually receive.

Three regulations turn this cadence from a habit into an obligation. The **CRA** requires a support period that reflects expected use and is at least 5 years, with free security updates kept separate from feature updates where feasible, and each update kept available for 10 years. Its main obligations apply from December 2027 ([CRA summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyber_Resilience_Act); [distr.sh](https://distr.sh/glossary/cra-support-period/)). The **EU ecodesign rule for phones and tablets** (Regulation 2023/1670, applying since 20 June 2025) requires OS updates for at least 5 years after the last unit of a model is placed on the market ([European Commission](https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/news/new-eu-rules-durable-energy-efficient-and-repairable-smartphones-and-tablets-start-applying-2025-06-20_en)). Because a model is sold for 1 to 2 years, that means 6 to 7 years of builds. **UN R156** requires a certified software update management system, a register of software versions and integrity data per RXSWIN, and a record of every update. Its 01 series, adopted November 2025, makes RXSWIN effectively mandatory ([UNECE R156](https://unece.org/sites/default/files/2024-03/R156e%20(2).pdf)). R156 already defines a versioned, audited "release build" for cars, which is a ready-made billing unit. The CRA's demand for separate, free security updates also means a pricing model must never make a security fix cost the customer extra.

| Segment | Public release builds (evidence) | Multiplier | Regulatory driver |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Flagship phone | ~12-16 per model per year (estimate from Samsung tiers), 7 years | Regions, carriers, dozens of models | Ecodesign 2023/1670, CRA |
| Tesla-cadence car | 165 versions in 12 months | Many ECU images per release | UN R156, R155 |
| Legacy carmaker | ~3-4 major releases per year (XPeng, Mercedes, VW) | ECUs × platforms | UN R156, R155 |
| Drone | ~4-5 per model per year (DJI Mini 4 Pro) | Aircraft plus controller per model | CRA |
| Robot vacuum | Multi-model waves (7 models at once) | Models × regions | CRA |
| E-bike drive system | Several feature updates per year (Bosch) | Motor, battery, display | CRA, EN 15194 anti-tampering |

## No protection vendor bills per build, and buyers punish surprise bills

App-protection vendors price **per app, per platform, per year**, and none publishes a rate card. Vendr's small sample puts Guardsquare's average contract at about **$44,335 a year**, with a high of about $83,000 ([Vendr](https://www.vendr.com/buyer-guides/guardsquare-nv)). Capterra lists Appdome from **$50,000 a year** ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/249627/Appdome/)). The only public metered price is Denuvo on AWS Marketplace: **$25,000 per game per month plus $0.50 per activation** ([AWS Marketplace](https://aws.amazon.com/marketplace/pp/prodview-x443idlstvufi)). Denuvo is also the protection most visibly removed after launch on cost grounds ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/drm/why-are-pc-developers-removing-denuvo)). At the low end, desktop packers sell one-off licences from EUR 249 to $999 per developer ([tech-insider.org](https://tech-insider.org/denuvo-vs-vmprotect-vs-themida-2026/)). Embedded security stacks such as SEGGER's are royalty-free ([SEGGER](https://www.segger.com/purchase/licensing/)). Emproof sells editions on subscription, with a mandatory onboarding package for every licence and no published price or billing unit ([Emproof service description](https://emproof.com/en/service-description)).

Two vendors already meter something close to builds, but always as a cap inside an annual contract. Jscrambler's trial unit is the "protection" run ([Jscrambler FAQ](https://jscrambler.com/frequently-asked-questions)). Its plans vary by the number of protections, and one user warned that "if you have many builds and environments, you must watch your usage" ([GetApp](https://www.getapp.co.uk/software/101685/jscrambler)). An old Appdome price sheet included **"10 App updates per year"** in a per-app fee ([Appdome PDF](https://www.appdome.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Appdome-for-App-Publishers-Pricing.pdf)). The closest precedent for one artifact as one unit is code signing. DigiCert counts **1,000 signatures per certificate**, and "each binary or artifact signed consumes one signature unit" ([DigiCert](https://docs.digicert.com/en/digicert-keylocker/overview/licensing.html)). Azure includes 5,000 or 100,000 signatures a month, with overage at $0.005 each ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/apps/package-and-deploy/code-signing-options)). That works out to cents per unit. A per-build obfuscation price in the hundreds of dollars therefore has **no direct precedent** and must be justified by value, not cost. Coverity Scan rationed builds by code size, from 28 a week below 100K lines to 7 a week above 1M ([Coverity FAQ](https://scan.coverity.com/faq)). That is a precedent for scaling by code size, and so for scaling by share of functions protected. Veracode moved the other way and sells per application with **unlimited scans** ([aicodereview.cc](https://aicodereview.cc/blog/veracode-pricing/)).

Buyer acceptance is the main risk. GitHub announced a per-minute fee on self-hosted runners in December 2025 and **postponed it after one day** of community backlash ([GitHub changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2025-12-16-coming-soon-simpler-pricing-and-a-better-experience-for-github-actions/); [GitHub community](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/182089)). Pricing commentators agree that pure consumption pricing creates "unpredictable costs that make budget planning difficult," and they recommend a base fee with included usage and soft limits ([Monetizely](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/how-to-price-developer-tools-feature-gating-strategies-and-tier-design-for-code-quality-platforms-ed4ae)). Metered security tools have a worse problem: customers optimise against the meter. Coverity told users to scan "but not on each and every commit," and a per-build fee taxes exactly the frequent security releases that the CRA demands. Scaling price by the share of functions protected is easier to accept, because the customer knows that figure at contract time.

## A per-release ruler inside an annual commitment

The model below keeps the user's idea, one protected release build as one unit priced by protection scope, and wraps it in the structure the market accepts. **All numbers are our assumptions, chosen to sit below the Guardsquare and Appdome anchors for small customers and well below Denuvo for large ones. They are not validated with any buyer.**

**The unit.** A billable unit is one binary or firmware image for one model or ECU variant that receives a release version ID and ships to customers or enters a regulated register (for cars, the R156 RXSWIN record). Rebuilding the same version ID within 14 days does not count again. Internal CI and developer builds are unlimited and free. They use a development profile that the licence does not allow to be shipped, so protection never sits behind a meter in the developer loop.

**The price driver.** The tool reports, at build time, the share of functions protected. The contract sets a scope tier: **Focus** (up to 5% of functions, such as one limiter or one navigation module) at $250 per build; **Standard** (5-25%) at $500; **Broad** (above 25%) at $900. Volume steps apply across the year: builds 1-50 at list, 51-200 at 70%, and 201 and above at 40%.

**The guardrails.** Security-only releases count as half a unit, so CRA-mandated fixes are never rationed. The annual minimum is $12,000, prepaid against the customer's release plan. Unused units roll over for 12 months. Overage is trued up once a year, and a release is never blocked. A **cap of $30,000 per protected model or ECU line per year** at Standard scope turns very high cadence into a flat fee. There is a one-time onboarding fee of $8,000, following Emproof's mandatory onboarding package.

| | A. E-bike drive-unit maker | B. Robot-vacuum maker | C. Tesla-cadence carmaker | C'. Legacy carmaker (contrast) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Protected lines (assumption) | 3 controllers (2 motors, 1 display/battery) | 7 models × 2 regional firmware variants | 6 protected ECU images | 6 ECUs × 8 platforms |
| Releases (assumption, from evidence) | 4 feature + 2 security per line per year | 5 per model per year (DJI/Roborock range 3-6), 1 of them security-only | 165 vehicle releases (Tessie); each ECU image changes in half of them; 20% security-only | 4 per year |
| Scope | Focus (assist map, speed limiter) | Standard (navigation, cleaning logic) | Standard | Standard |
| Billable units | 12 + 6 × 0.5 = **15** | 56 + 14 × 0.5 = **63** | 396 + 99 × 0.5 ≈ **446** | **192** |
| Calculation | 15 × $250 = $3,750, below the minimum | 50 × $500 + 13 × $350 | 50 × $500 + 150 × $350 + 246 × $200 | 50 × $500 + 142 × $350 |
| **Annual price** | **$12,000** (minimum) + $8,000 onboarding in year 1 | **$29,550** | **$126,700** (cap $180,000 not reached) | **$74,700** |
| If every ECU image is rebuilt each release | n/a | n/a | 891 units = $215,700, so the **$180,000 cap applies** | n/a |
| Comparison | Below Guardsquare's ~$44k average per app | Below Guardsquare ~$44k and Appdome $50k+ | Under half of one Denuvo title-year ($300k + $0.50/unit) | Above one Guardsquare app, below two |

Three things show up in the examples. First, the minimum carries the small customer: an e-bike supplier pays a flat fee, and per-build counting is only a report. That is acceptable, but it means small segments bring about $12k a year each. Second, the vacuum maker's bill is driven by **model and region breadth**, not cadence, which is the intended result: price follows the size of the protected portfolio. Third, Tesla-like cadence would explode a pure per-build bill, from $127k to $216k depending on an assumption we cannot check. The per-line cap is what makes the model safe to sign. In practice the large customer will negotiate a flat enterprise fee. Per-build counting then becomes the true-up ruler and the evidence trail for R156 and CRA audits. The compliance angle may be worth more than the price unit: a signed, per-release record of what was protected, and to what measured time-to-break, is evidence a maker can attach to its R156 register or CRA technical file.

## Conclusion

Cloning and cadence pull in opposite directions as sales arguments. Cloning is the more emotive story, but its documented money sits with insiders, and its shipped-product version cannot be proven or priced from public records. Cadence is dull, but it is measurable, regulated and growing: every model a maker supports produces release builds for 5 to 7 years by law. That reshapes the product. The thing we sell per release is not "protection from Chinese clones." It is **a protected, measured, audit-ready release**, which fits R156 registers, CRA technical files and Euro 7 or EN 15194 anti-tampering declarations, and it supports the earlier finding that anti-tampering should lead. A per-build price works only as a ruler inside a commitment. If we let it act as a meter, it taxes the security releases the law requires, and buyers will see that.

**Verdict:** do not pitch anti-cloning as a documented, costly problem. Mention it only as "your patents keep failing, so protect the know-how in the binary," aimed at makers of robot-vacuum-class algorithms, and say clearly that insider theft needs other controls. Adopt the per-release unit with scope tiers, minimum, security discount and per-line cap as the pricing hypothesis. Lead with anti-tampering compliance. The cheapest validation steps, in order:

1. **One afternoon on the gaps.** Search China Judgments Online for unfair-competition or software-copyright cases between Chinese device makers, and count one year of factory images for one Pixel and one Roborock model. A single shipped-firmware copying case would revive the cloning pitch; the counts would firm up the cadence assumptions.
2. **Three pricing conversations with one sheet.** Show the worked-example table to one e-bike controller supplier, one robot-vacuum or drone maker, and one automotive Tier-1 software lead. Ask which unit they would budget against: release builds, product lines or a flat fee. Also ask how many release builds they actually shipped last year. If two of three choose a flat per-line fee, drop per-build as the headline and keep it as the audit report.
3. **One question on copying in the same calls.** "When a rival matched one of your algorithms, did it come through people, patents, or someone studying your shipped device?" If "studying the device" comes up at least twice, anti-copying earns a second slot in the pitch. Otherwise it stays vision only.
