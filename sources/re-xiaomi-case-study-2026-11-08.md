# Xiaomi bought speed, never stole algorithms

*Synthesised 2026-11-08 from the three research notes in `research_notes/Xiaomi case study/`, building on `sources/re-device-cloning-build-cadence-pricing-report-2026-11-08.md` and `sources/re-machine-software-cloning-report-2026-11-08.md`. Business, legal and policy level only; no technical attack or bypass methods. Not legal advice.*

**No. Xiaomi did not steal the technology, and no court anywhere has found that it copied another company's software, firmware or algorithms.** The design-copying charge is executive rhetoric — Jony Ive's "it is theft and it is lazy," October 2014 — that Apple never took to court, and the one case Apple did bring and win was about the *name* "Mi Pad," which only blocked an EU trademark registration ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2014/10/09/apples-jony-ive-is-not-flattered-by-xiaomi/); [9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2017/12/05/xiaomi-mi-pad-trademark/)). Every significant patent case against Xiaomi is a royalty or FRAND dispute about the price of a licence, not an allegation that Xiaomi took anything; the only genuine software issue is a long record of late GPLv2 kernel-source releases plus a single 2014 incident where MIUI shipped a renamed fork of the open-source Xposed framework with attribution stripped, which Xiaomi apologised for and remedied ([Gizmochina](https://www.gizmochina.com/2023/04/24/xiaomi-kernel-source-code-gpl-violation/); [Greenbot](https://www.greenbot.com/xiaomi-forked-xposed-framework-miui-without-giving-credit-developer/)). Xiaomi's speed has a mundane explanation: it took minority stakes of **20–40%** in partner companies that designed and built the products while Xiaomi supplied the brand, the channel and the first customers — Roborock's Xiaomi-related revenue ran **100.0%, then 90.4%, then 50.2%** across three years before its IPO ([EqualOcean](https://equalocean.com/analysis/201905031993)). Xiaomi did not outspend anyone either: its 2025 R&D of RMB 33.1bn is roughly **one seventh of Apple's US$34.6bn** ([BusinessQuant](https://businessquant.com/metrics/aapl/research-and-development)). The useful finding for anyone building a company here is not that Xiaomi cheated, but the opposite — the hardware is already a commodity that a brand can buy as a finished ODM design in weeks for tens of thousands of dollars, while the incumbent's algorithm took years and tens of millions, which makes the algorithm inside the shipped device the last asymmetry an incumbent still holds.

## No court has found Xiaomi copied software, firmware or an algorithm

Say this first and say it plainly, because the alternative is a judge catching you repeating a slogan. Across English-language court reporting and specialist legal press there is **no trade-secret misappropriation case and no reverse-engineering case against Xiaomi in any jurisdiction**. What exists instead falls into three piles, and the distinction between them is where the whole argument lives.

The first pile is rhetoric. Apple's then design chief called Xiaomi's work "theft" and "lazy" at a Vanity Fair summit on 9 October 2014, remarks that reporting at the time read as aimed at copycats generally, Samsung included ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2014/10/09/apples-jony-ive-is-not-flattered-by-xiaomi/); [Daring Fireball](https://daringfireball.net/linked/2014/10/09/ive-xiaomi)). Xiaomi's executives answered in kind — Hugo Barra told WSJDLive that "the iPhone 6 carries design language that is very HTC-like" ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2014-10-29-xiaomi-says-its-not-copying-apple-but-apple-is-copying-htc.html)) — and the press documented close visual and specification similarity, most bluntly on the Mi Pad, whose 7.9-inch 2,048 × 1,536 panel matched the iPad mini 2 and which Xiaomi's own marketing compared to Apple's ([AppleInsider](https://appleinsider.com/articles/14/05/15/chinas-xiaomi-unveils-copycat-mi-pad-in-bid-to-take-on-apples-ipad)). None of it was ever litigated as design or trade-dress infringement. **Apple's choice to sue over the name and not the design is itself the evidence**: a company that took Samsung through years of design litigation had both the resources and the appetite, and it did not bring that case here.

The second pile is patent and standards licensing, which is routinely misreported as theft. Ericsson's December 2014 Indian suit over eight standards-essential patents produced the widely cited "a court banned Xiaomi phones" headline — an *ex parte* interim injunction that the Delhi High Court later vacated in part, in April 2016, on a finding that **Ericsson had concealed material information**, namely that Xiaomi's 3G implementation was already licensed through Qualcomm, itself an Ericsson licensee ([SpicyIP](https://spicyip.com/2016/04/dhc-rules-that-ericsson-concealed-relevant-information-in-xiaomi-matter-vacates-injunction-in-part.html)). The rest of the pattern is unremarkable for any implementer scaling internationally: licences with InterDigital in August 2021, Sisvel and Mitsubishi Electric in June 2021 over 120-plus patent families, and Philips in 2022 ending litigation across four countries ([JUVE Patent](https://www.juve-patent.com/people-and-business/interdigital-and-xiaomi-sign-licensing-agreement/); [Sisvel](https://www.sisvel.com/news/xiaomi-sisvel-agree-license-cellular-SEPs/); [JUVE Patent](https://www.juve-patent.com/cases/philips-and-xiaomi-settle-dispute-over-umts-and-lte-technology/)). Xiaomi also plays offence, having destroyed a KPN patent at first instance in the German Federal Patent Court ([JUVE Patent](https://www.juve-patent.com/cases/xiaomi-destroys-kpn-patent-with-bardehle-in-first-instance/)). A standards-essential patent dispute is a disagreement about the price of a licence to technology an implementer is *required* to use to be standards-compliant. It is a commercial argument, and a judge will know that.

The third pile is where Xiaomi genuinely has a record, and it is narrow. XDA and others have documented repeated late or missing GPLv2 kernel-source releases against Xiaomi's own stated three-month target, including roughly eleven devices unreleased a year after launch as of April 2023, with open issues still live on Xiaomi's public kernel repository ([XDA](https://www.xda-developers.com/gplv2-and-its-infringement-by-xiaomi/); [Gizmochina](https://www.gizmochina.com/2023/04/24/xiaomi-kernel-source-code-gpl-violation/); [MiCode issue #41153](https://github.com/MiCode/Xiaomi_Kernel_OpenSource/issues/41153)). This is a failure to publish modifications to code that is already free — a licence-compliance breach, not misappropriation, and no court has adjudicated it. The 2014 Xposed/Dexspy episode is the single closest thing to "shipped someone else's code": MIUI carried a fork of the open-source Xposed framework renamed so the original name no longer appeared and with the authors' credits deleted; Xiaomi apologised, said it was debugging code not meant for release, removed it and credited the original developer ([Greenbot](https://www.greenbot.com/xiaomi-forked-xposed-framework-miui-without-giving-credit-developer/)). Open code, admitted, remedied, no secret involved, no litigation.

One trap to avoid on stage: do not borrow the Dyson disputes. Dyson's fights are with **Dreame and Roborock, which are separate companies that sue each other**, and Dyson has lost badly in one of them — German courts at first and second instance found no infringement on the Dreame V9/V10 and barred Dyson from writing to Dreame's customers ([ChinaIPToday](http://www.chinaiptoday.com/post.html?id=1461)). Attributing an investee's litigation to Xiaomi is a category error a judge will spot in one question.

The ladder is deliberate rather than random: accessory, then screen, then body, then home, then mobility, then compute. And the one category Xiaomi could not hand to a partner is the one that proves the point. That is fast the expensive way. **If copying were the mechanism, the car would have been the cheapest product in the catalogue instead of the most expensive.**

## Minority stakes and a guaranteed order book, not a stolen blueprint

Xiaomi's range was built mostly by other companies, under a structure that is openly documented and, in two cases, laid out in the partners' own IPO filings. Lei Jun pledged in 2013 to invest in a hundred hardware companies in five years; by May 2015 the count was about 39, and investments ran to **several million RMB for 20–40% stakes, deliberately never a controlling one** ([EE Times](https://www.eetimes.com/xiaomis-spending-spree-its-logic-and-method/)). Xiaomi's 2018 IPO prospectus discloses an ecosystem of **more than 210 companies**, over 90 of them doing IoT and smart-hardware R&D, with 300 SKUs across 194 interoperable categories ([IPO prospectus](http://cnbj1.fds.api.xiaomi.com/company/financial/en-US/IPO.pdf)). By March 2020 the figure was **more than 300 investee companies** at RMB 32.3bn book value, and president Wang Xiang said later that year that **roughly 120 of them actually make ecosystem products** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/05/22/xiaomi-investment-portfolio-surpasses-300-companies); [Caixin Global](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2020-12-11/qa-xiaomi-president-wang-xiang-explains-how-to-build-a-business-ecosystem-101638423.html)). That ~120 figure is the honest one to quote for "who builds the products."

The mechanism is not capital, it is demand underwriting. **Roborock's Xiaomi-related revenue was 100.0% of total in the first of three disclosed years, then 90.4%, then 50.2%; Ninebot's was 55.6%, 73.7%, 57.3% of gross revenue.** Both flagged Xiaomi as an operating risk precisely because it was simultaneously shareholder, largest customer and sales channel, and in the Roborock split Xiaomi supplied brand, channel and first users while Roborock did the navigation, engineering and supply chain ([EqualOcean](https://equalocean.com/analysis/201905031993)). A partner entering a new category therefore started with a guaranteed order book and a built distribution network, which removes the two slowest, riskiest parts of any hardware launch: proving demand and building a channel. Xiaomi converted its phone user base into an underwriting instrument for other people's products. The board-level resolution capping hardware net margin at **5.0%** from 2018 is what made the trade worth it to the partner, since it let any new category launch at a price incumbents could not match ([IPO prospectus](http://cnbj1.fds.api.xiaomi.com/company/financial/en-US/IPO.pdf)). Peer-reviewed work describes the same thing in neutral language: "investment + incubation" that "greatly shortened Xiaomi's time to the IoT market" ([Procedia Computer Science](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1877050922000084/pdf)).

The model is built to graduate partners, not hold them, which is why it is not a control story. Xiaomi-linked ownership of Roborock fell from roughly 24.7% at its 2020 IPO to **2.49%**, with the founder now the controlling shareholder ([RevenueMemo](https://www.revenuememo.com/p/who-owns-roborock)). Roborock and Dreame now compete with Xiaomi-branded vacuums.

Two numbers finish off the "they must have cheated" instinct. First, Xiaomi now does serious R&D of its own, rising from about RMB 7bn in 2019 to **RMB 33.1bn in 2025**, with 25,457 R&D staff at year-end and over 45,000 granted patents ([2025 annual results](https://ir.mi.com/system/files-encrypted/nasdaq_kms/assets/2026/03/24/5-35-03/25Q4%20EN%20AC%20Xiaomi.pdf)). Second, and more usefully, **it still spends about one seventh of what Apple does** — RMB 33.1bn is roughly US$4.6bn at 7.2 RMB/USD against Apple's FY2025 US$34.55bn and Samsung's 2024 ₩35 trillion (~US$24.1bn) ([BusinessQuant](https://businessquant.com/metrics/aapl/research-and-development); [Korea Herald](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10439155)). The speed story is not "Xiaomi outspends incumbents." It is "Xiaomi spends less and still ships more categories." Expansion was not even monotonic: group revenue fell from RMB 328.3bn in 2021 to 280.0bn in 2022 and 271.0bn in 2023 before reaching 457.3bn in 2025 ([2025 annual report](https://ir.mi.com/system/files-encrypted/nasdaq_kms/assets/2026/04/28/5-29-08/Xiaomi%202025%20AR_EN.pdf)).

## Ninety days on a pre-certified platform against Dyson's four years and $71m

Here is the reframe, and it is both true and stronger than any accusation. The slow, expensive part of a consumer device is no longer the hardware. Published explanations of Chinese fast-follower speed describe an **ecosystem property, not superior invention**: chip vendors bundling circuit-board layouts, component lists and OS source into turnkey reference designs, ODMs selling complete productised platforms, public moulds, and a component cluster where Shenzhen's own government claims over 90% of a smart device's core components can be sourced within an hour ([Shanzhai Revolution](https://richard367.substack.com/p/shanzhai-revolution-how-chinas-grassroots); [Shenzhen Government Online](https://www.sz.gov.cn/en_szgov/news/latest/content/post_12950959.html)). In the shanzhai era this cut a 12–18 month handset programme to **four to six weeks**, and academic work credits MediaTek's turnkey platform with shortening lead time to market from about nine months to roughly three ([Wikipedia, Shanzhai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanzhai); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249367843_Shanzhai_manufacturing_-_an_alternative_innovation_phenomenon_in_China_Its_value_chain_and_implications_for_Chinese_science_and_technology_policies)). That machinery migrated upward into branded ODM sourcing, and Huaqiangbei today sources more than a hundred component types in a day and produces prototypes within a week ([ECNS](https://www.ecns.cn/cns-wire/2026-08-06/detail-ihfhziqh0063782.shtml)).

Against that, look at what the signature capability inside the incumbent's product actually cost.

The ratio is roughly **16–20x on time and three orders of magnitude on engineering cost**, and the top-row figures come from the incumbents themselves while the bottom-row figures come from suppliers selling the service — treat the second group as evidence of what is *sold*, not as neutral measurement. The structural version of the same point is tier-1 consultancy data: McKinsey's benchmarking of over 1,600 IC design projects found more than 80% late by nearly 30% on average, and that on-time projects **reuse more existing content** ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/how-predictive-analytics-can-boost-product-development)). Schedule compression comes from reused design content, not from faster invention.

That is why the ODM supplying the navigation platform and the IoT app layer matters so much: a fast follower's product can match features **without the follower ever having written the algorithm**. The same navigation platform turns up under unrelated Western brands — Shenzhen Picea Robotics, formerly 3irobotix, is the robot-vacuum ODM behind products for SharkNinja and Anker's Eufy among others ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shenzhen_Picea_Robotics)). Sustained in-house R&D, meanwhile, is a cost base the turnkey buyer never carries: Dyson spent a record **£468m (£9m a week)** on new products in 2023 with around 6,500 engineers, and iRobot's R&D fell from **$166.5m in FY2022 to $93.3m in FY2024** while faster-moving competitors gained share ([City AM](https://www.cityam.com/dyson-powers-up-tech-investment-after-hitting-record-global-sales/); [iRobot FY2024 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1159167/000115916725000011/irbt-20241228.htm)).

**So the algorithm inside the shipped device is the last asymmetry an incumbent still has.** The reason it survived this long is not law — studying a lawfully purchased product is an accepted route to a trade secret in both the EU and the US, and the US DTSA excludes reverse engineering from "improper means" ([Directive 2016/943, Art. 3](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2016/943/oj); [18 U.S.C. 1839](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1839)). The reason is cost: reading an algorithm out of firmware used to be slow, specialised work. AI agents are what changes that economics, and the evidence here is still thin and vendor-supplied — Thales reports an AI agent finding 8 of 10 vulnerabilities in an unprotected app and none in the protected one, after 970 times the token consumption, in a release that names neither agent nor model, while practitioners on public forums say current models undo basic obfuscation and one says AI helps people who already have the skill rather than replacing it ([Thales](https://cpl.thalesgroup.com/about-us/newsroom/sentinel-envelope-plus-ai-reverse-engineering-protection)). Treat that as direction, not proof.

### The honest limits, stated before a judge finds them

**Nobody has yet documented a competitor extracting an algorithm from a shipped consumer device.** Across this research and the two earlier reports, the large proven losses from copied device software all ran through insiders carrying source code — Motorola v. Hytera, ASML v. XTAL, Sinovel — and the consumer-device rivalries are patent and design cases in which the incumbent often loses on invalidity. The single clean case of a rival profiting from a maker's shipped software is Ubiquiti v. Cambium, settled for an undisclosed sum. The turnkey argument is also **strongest for market entry and for the long tail of brands, and weakest about today's category leaders**: Dreame's own history begins with high-speed digital motor work in 2015, two years before the company was founded, and both Dreame and Roborock now run substantial in-house R&D ([PandaYoo](https://pandayoo.com/post/dreame-technology-the-full-story-of-a-chinese-tech-challenger/)). And all of this rests on **English-language sources only**; Chinese- and Korean-language dockets were not searched natively, so "no trade-secret case exists" must be said as "none found in English-language court reporting and specialist legal press as of 2026-11-08."

Four verification caveats to carry openly. **One:** ir.mi.com returned HTTP 403, so the 2025 Xiaomi figures (R&D RMB 33.1bn, 25,457 R&D staff, 45,000+ patents, revenue RMB 457.3bn) come from search summaries of the primary announcement rather than from reading the filing, and should be re-verified before being quoted as exact filings data. **Two:** no source links chip-vendor reference designs to Xiaomi's launch speed specifically — the reference-design mechanism is documented for the shanzhai handset ecosystem generally, and **must not be asserted about Xiaomi**. **Three:** the Mi Band and Mi Air Purifier launched in **2014** (July and December); one source dates both to 2015, and 2014 is correct on contemporaneous launch coverage. **Four:** the widely quoted "400+ ecosystem companies" mixes Lei Jun's personal angel investments with Xiaomi Technology, the Hubei Xiaomi Yangtze River Industry Fund and Shunwei Capital, a much wider perimeter than Xiaomi's own count — say "over 300 investee companies, of which roughly 120 actually make the products" ([EE Times](https://www.eetimes.com/xiaomis-spending-spree-its-logic-and-method/)).

## What to say on stage, and what to say when challenged

Three lines the presenter can use verbatim:

> "No court anywhere has ever found that Xiaomi copied another company's software, firmware or an algorithm. We checked. The copying story is a 2014 quote from Apple's design chief that Apple never took to court."

> "Xiaomi did not outspend anyone — it spends about a seventh of Apple's R&D. It took twenty to forty percent stakes in a hundred and twenty companies that built the products, and gave them a brand and a guaranteed order book. Roborock's first year of revenue was one hundred percent Xiaomi."

> "Dyson spent seventy-one million dollars, four years and a hundred and three engineers on one hair dryer. A competitor can buy a finished, pre-certified device design in ninety days for thirty thousand dollars of tooling. The hardware is a commodity. The algorithm is the only thing left — and that is what we protect."

If a judge asks "so did Xiaomi actually steal anything?", the answer is:

> "No, and that is the interesting part. On the public record there is no trade-secret case, no reverse-engineering case, and no finding of copied software, firmware or algorithms against Xiaomi anywhere. There are two real things: a long record of releasing GPL kernel sources late or not at all, which is a licence-compliance failure on code that is already free, and one 2014 incident where MIUI shipped a renamed fork of the open-source Xposed framework with the authors' credit removed — which Xiaomi apologised for and fixed. Everything else is either a patent royalty dispute about the price of a licence, or an opinion someone gave to a journalist. We are not here because Xiaomi stole anything. We are here because Xiaomi proved you no longer need to."

The thing not to say, under any framing: that Xiaomi stole the technology, that it reverse-engineered a competitor's firmware, or that any court found it copied software. There is no basis for any of those, and a judge who has read one Delhi High Court order will end the pitch.

## Conclusion

The Xiaomi story is a worse argument for anti-copying than it looks and a much better argument for the product than it looks. As an accusation it collapses on contact with the docket. As a case study in industrial structure it is close to perfect: a company reached almost every consumer category on one seventh of Apple's R&D by letting other people take the product risk and paying them in demand, and the only time it had to invent something end-to-end it spent RMB 13bn and three years doing it the way Dyson does. The lesson is not about ethics. It is that **the part of a consumer device that is hard to acquire has moved**. Chassis, tooling, radios, certification and even the app layer are purchasable in weeks; the decade of motor work, the twenty years of heart-rate research, the three years of SLAM tuning are not. Those live as compiled logic on a device the competitor can buy at retail, legally, in any market.

That reframing also fixes what can honestly be promised. The two earlier reports found that documented, court-proven losses from copied device software run through insiders, not teardowns, and that anti-tampering carries the regulatory deadlines and the measured harm. Nothing here overturns that. What Xiaomi adds is the *motive* evidence the anti-copying use case was missing: a demonstrated, industrial-scale mechanism for entering a category with everything except the algorithm, operating at brand scale and advertised openly by the suppliers who sell it. The remaining question is no longer whether an incumbent's algorithm is the valuable thing — the Dyson and Firstbeat numbers settle that — but whether the cost of reading it out of a shipped device is falling fast enough to matter this year. That is a measurable question, and measuring it is a better pitch than any accusation.
