# Firmware anti-tampering wins the pitch, not the pipeline

*Synthesised 2026-11-07 from the research notes in `research_notes/Vehicle firmware anti tampering case/`, plus a later check of Emproof's own pages. Business and policy level only; no attack or bypass techniques.*

Vehicle and machine controller firmware is the strongest *story* among our candidate first use cases, but it is a weaker *first customer* than it looks, and it only works if we pitch it as measured evidence layered on top of cryptographic protection rather than as obfuscation. The case for it is unusually well documented: EU law now obliges car makers to "minimise vulnerabilities" to tampering in engine and motor control units, with market-surveillance authorities attacking vehicles after sale using tuning products bought on the open market ([EUR-Lex 2025/1706](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32025R1706)); the US EPA counted more than 550,000 deleted diesel pickups ([EPA AED 2020](https://19january2021snapshot.epa.gov/sites/static/files/2021-01/documents/epaaedletterreportontampereddieselpickups.pdf)); and Dutch police found more than half of checked Amsterdam fatbikes tuned ([NOS](https://nos.nl/artikel/2527235-ruim-helft-gecontroleerde-fatbikes-in-amsterdam-opgevoerd)). Against that, no binding EU or UNECE text mentions obfuscation, the European cycling industry explicitly lists "reliance on obscurity alone" as *not* state of the art ([ECI position paper, Sep 2026](https://cyclingindustries.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Position-Paper-Anti-Tampering.pdf)), software for a traditional OEM vehicle starts 3 to 4 years before production ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/automotive-and-assembly/our-insights/winning-the-automotive-software-development-race)), and a funded competitor, Emproof, already claims proof-backed firmware obfuscation ("formal proofs such as translation validation") under ISO 26262 ([Emproof](https://emproof.com/en/solution)). Compared with PC games (biggest money story, entrenched incumbents) and paid professional software (faster buyers who already pay for protection), firmware is the better hackathon narrative and the slower business. Our verdict: pitch firmware, but make the differentiator the open AI-agent time-to-break measurement, which maps directly onto Euro 7's market-surveillance attack testing, not the proof; frame the product as "time-to-break evidence for the anti-tampering declaration"; and target second-tier e-bike drive-unit and controller suppliers and small L-category makers first.

## Tampering is measured, rising in two-wheelers, and concentrated in a few tool makers

The scale evidence is strongest in the US and in EU heavy trucks, and most visibly growing in micromobility. The EPA's 2020 analysis found emission controls removed from **more than 550,000 US diesel pickups (about 15% of the certified fleet)**, producing **more than 570,000 tons of lifetime excess NOx**, equivalent to adding 9 million compliant trucks; over half of deleted vehicles were tampered by age three, and EPA notes that deleting controls "almost always requires the use of some type of tune or tuner" ([EPA AED 2020](https://19january2021snapshot.epa.gov/sites/static/files/2021-01/documents/epaaedletterreportontampereddieselpickups.pdf)). A later EPA page rounds this to "over 500,000" and "about 13%", so the two EPA texts disagree slightly ([EPA tampering page](https://cleanairnortheast.epa.gov/tampering.html)). The same report identified **32 distinct delete-tune manufacturers** and more than 70 resolved civil cases covering over one million defeat devices.

In the EU there is no single measured tampering rate. Roadside checks find confirmed cheat devices in roughly **2-8% of heavy trucks**: UK DVSA found AdBlue emulators in about 2.9% of 10,237 trucks stopped in 2017-18 ([Commercial Motor](https://www.commercialmotor.com/news/article/dvsa-launches-national-adblue-cheat-crackdown-after-finding-significant-non)), and Germany's BAG found tampering in 4.1% of 7,070 checks in one reported year, only 19 of 292 cases on German-registered trucks ([eurotransport](https://www.eurotransport.de/artikel/adblue-betrug-so-sucht-das-bag-nach-adblue-emulatoren-10681172.html)). Remote sensing of 33,600 European trucks and buses flagged **7.6% of Euro VI trucks as high emitters**, without separating tampering from malfunction ([ICCT 2022](https://theicct.org/publication/remote-sensing-of-heavy-duty-vehicle-emissions-in-europe/)). For cars, TNO found about **5-7% of Dutch diesel cars with the particle filter removed or defective** ([TNO](https://m.tno.nl/en/focus-areas/traffic-transport/roadmaps/sustainable-traffic-and-transport/sustainable-mobility-and-logistics/improving-air-quality-by-monitoring-real-world-emissions/emissions-of-particulate-matter-from-diesel-cars/)), with a lower 2019 estimate of 1.5% ([TNO 2019](https://legacy.emissieregistratie.nl/erpubliek/documenten/05%20Verkeer%20en%20vervoer/2019%20(TNO)%20Emission%20compliance%20over%20the%20lifespan%20of%20a%20vehicle.pdf)). The Euro 7 impact assessment's public consultation named tampering as a driver of real-world emissions in **117 of 124 replies**, but the IA's own modelled marginal benefit from tamper protection is small (a few kilotonnes of NOx), so the case for buyers is legal compliance, not a large modelled environmental saving ([SWD(2022) 359](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:52022SC0359); [Part 2](https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2022-11/SWD_2022_359_1_EN_impact_assessment_part2_v5.pdf)). Pitch figures above 20% come from pre-selected samples or NGOs and should not be used.

Two-wheelers and e-bikes are where speed tuning is most visible and growing. Amsterdam police found **117 of 215 checked fatbikes (54%) tuned** in early 2024, and about **10,500 fatbikes were seized nationally in 2024** ([NOS](https://nos.nl/artikel/2527235-ruim-helft-gecontroleerde-fatbikes-in-amsterdam-opgevoerd); [Risk & Business](https://riskenbusiness.nl/nieuws/markt/politie-nam-in-2024-ruim-10-000-fatbikes-in-beslag-na-opvoercontrole/)). Baden-Württemberg detected 363 manipulated pedelecs against 291 the year before ([Pforzheimer Zeitung](https://www.pz-news.de/baden-wuerttemberg_artikel,-Getunte-E-Scooter-und-Co-Warnung-vor-wachsender-Gefahr-_arid,2374171.html)), and German police estimate 5-10% of e-bikes are tuned ([Polizei Dein Partner](https://www.polizei-dein-partner.de/infos-fuer/autofahrer/detailansicht-autofahrer/artikel/e-bike-tuning-illegal-und-gefaehrlich.html)). No source isolates tuned vehicles in accident statistics, and the Dutch fatbike problem is largely cheap imported bikes rather than big-brand drive systems, so tuning is not spread evenly across makers. The market is about **5.1 million e-bikes sold in Europe per year** ([CONEBI](https://www.conebi.eu/pr-conebi-bimp-2024/)), with decision power concentrated in four or five drive-unit makers, Bosch at roughly half of Europe by an unaudited industry estimate ([ebike24](https://www.ebike24.com/blog/manufacturers-of-ebike-motors)). L-category is smaller: about 1.0 million motorcycles and 142,000 mopeds in the largest markets in 2025 ([ACEM](https://www.acem.eu/registrations-of-motorcycles-and-mopeds-in-key-european-markets-during-the-year-2025/)).

The tuning trade itself has no credible size. Report-mill estimates range from USD 0.8 billion for Europe to USD 9.8 billion globally with definitions varying fourfold ([IGTPS](https://www.igtps.com/report/europe-chip-tuning-market); [Dataintelo](https://dataintelo.com/report/chip-tuning-services-market)); the useful fact is structural: a handful of specialist tool makers supply the tools, and enforcement records show single US tuners selling tens to hundreds of thousands of units, such as Derive Systems' roughly 363,000 products ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/department-justice-epa-settle-derive-systems-over-aftermarket-emissions-defeat-devices)). The US enforcement climate has also reversed: DOJ stopped criminal prosecution of diesel tampering in January 2026 ([Truckinginfo](https://www.truckinginfo.com/news/justice-department-pulls-back-on-criminal-prosecution-of-diesel-emissions-delete)) and a June 2026 presidential memorandum told EPA to consider deprioritising civil enforcement against individuals ([White House](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/06/lowering-the-cost-of-living-by-promoting-the-freedom-to-fix/)). The "why now" is European, not American.

Three findings follow. First, **compliance is shown by process, but enforcement is by attack**: Euro 7's market-surveillance test selects vehicles partly on "evidence that effective tampering products are widely available on the market" and grades whether real attacks succeed ([EUR-Lex 2025/1706](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32025R1706)). A measured time-to-break maps directly onto that logic, which is the strongest argument for our metric. Second, **no binding text mentions obfuscation**: full-text searches of Euro 7, 2025/1706, UN R155 and the CRA for "obfusc" and "reverse engineer" returned nothing. The named examples are update authentication, integrity checks, secure boot and tamper-resistant hardware; the closest official wording is "encrypted software" in the Commission's CLOVE study ([CLOVE study](https://circabc.europa.eu/d/d/workspace/SpacesStore/8eb197c6-af2f-4d3d-8449-79906862f7db/%5b4%5d%20CLOVE%20-%20Anti-tampering%2c%20security%20and%20cybersecurity%20systems%20in%20Euro%207%20LDV%20implementing%20rules.pdf.pdf)). Obfuscation is neither required nor excluded; R155 §7.3.4 lets an OEM choose "another appropriate mitigation" if its TARA justifies it, so the product must be sold as a complement that protects integrity-check code and keys and as a source of evidence, not as the primary control. Third, the **ECI position is the most specific buyer spec found and it cuts against us**: it defines state of the art as tampering "not reasonably possible using commonly available tools and methods", demands signed updates with anti-rollback, cryptographically protected parameters, component authentication, locked debug interfaces and tamper-resistant logging, says "e-motor manufacturers must actively participate", and lists "reliance on obscurity alone" as not state of the art ([ECI](https://cyclingindustries.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Position-Paper-Anti-Tampering.pdf)). It also asks for tamper-proof data logging for police, which is an evidence product a firmware-integrity vendor could fit.

Right to repair sets a hard boundary. EU law separates protected tampering from legitimate repair by purpose and accreditation, not technology: OEMs must give independent operators dealer-equivalent diagnostic and reprogramming access through a standardised, certificate-based facility, with reprogramming over ISO 22900-2 or SAE J2534 ([EUR-Lex 2018/858](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32018R0858)), and Euro 7 Art 4(9) bars withholding parts-activation tools on anti-tampering grounds unless the OEM proves proportionality ([EUR-Lex 2024/1257](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1257)). Tractor software may be protected only if repair-relevant functions are equally open to independents ([EUR-Lex 167/2013](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32013R0167)). In the US, the FTC's July 2026 Deere settlement requires dealer-equivalent repair resources including reprogramming and "restarting a machine following an emissions-related shutdown" ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/07/ftc-states-secure-settlement-deere-company-advancing-farmers-right-repair)), while Colorado's law excludes modifications that break emissions or safety compliance ([Colorado HB23-1011](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/hb23-1011)). The EU Right to Repair Directive does not cover vehicles ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/columnists/2026/09/14/europes-right-to-repair-rules-are-broken-not-beaten/5295763)). The implication is precise: our protection must guard calibrations and limit logic against unauthorised change while leaving authenticated repair paths untouched. Machinery is the riskiest segment here, because OEM lockdown is under active antitrust attack.

## Incumbents own secure boot, tuning outlasts each lock, and Emproof already claims the proof

The competitive field has three layers. HSM silicon and firmware (Infineon AURIX, ETAS/ESCRYPT CycurHSM, Vector MICROSAR HSM) provides secure boot, signed flashing and key storage, ships in "millions of vehicles", and ETAS's distributor explicitly names tuning as a target ([Macnica/ETAS](https://www.macnica.co.jp/en/business/semiconductor/manufacturers/etas/products/140509/); [ETAS flyer](https://www.etas.com/ww/media/etas-flyer-escrypt-cycurhsm-en-230704.pdf)). Vehicle cyber platforms (PlaxidityX, Upstream, VicOne, Karamba, C2A, Cybellum, AUTOCRYPT) sell R155 compliance, intrusion detection and fleet monitoring, and AUTOCRYPT now brands itself "Your Partner for EURO 7 Compliance" ([AUTOCRYPT](https://www.autocrypt.io/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/AUTOCRYPT-EURO-7-Compliance_compressed.pdf)). Software-protection vendors mostly target apps and infotainment; Irdeto Cloakware for Automotive is the one visible obfuscation offer for in-vehicle software, with public material dating from 2016-2019 ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/cloakware-for-automotive-by-irdeto-receives-frost--sullivan-award-for-new-product-innovation-300386757.html)). The direct competitor is **Emproof**, which does binary-level firmware obfuscation for Arm, RISC-V and x86, raised a EUR 2 million seed in 2022 and an undisclosed round in March 2026, and names no commercial customers ([Silicon Canals](https://siliconcanals.com/emproof-secures-2m/); [FinSMEs](https://www.finsmes.com/2026/03/emproof-closes-funding-round.html); [HTGF](https://www.htgf.de/en/htgf-investment-emproof/)).

**The proof is not our differentiator.** The research notes initially disagreed: one pass, built on search summaries, found no vendor claiming that protection leaves controller behaviour unchanged, while another found a 2025 EEJournal article reporting that Emproof uses "translation validation via SMT solvers as part of their ISO 26262 (ASIL B) safety compliance", protecting only selected functions with a claimed 10-20% overhead ([EEJournal](https://www.eejournal.com/article/from-code-chaos-to-cyber-confidence-inside-the-world-of-firmware-obfuscation/)). A follow-up check of Emproof's own pages resolves this. Its solution page states "we use formal proofs such as translation validation to ensure the correctness of our translations" and claims ISO 26262 adherence ([Emproof solution](https://emproof.com/en/solution)), and its service description says the Nyx product is engineered for ISO 26262 up to ASIL D, with a process assessed by TÜV Nord, ASIL D only in the Enterprise edition ([Emproof service description](https://emproof.com/en/service-description)). The ASIL B (EEJournal) versus ASIL D (Emproof) difference is probably edition- or date-dependent, and all of it is vendor claim rather than independent audit; what exactly is proven per build remains unknown. The business conclusion does not depend on those details: **a direct, funded competitor already sells proof-backed firmware obfuscation into automotive safety contexts**, so "the only protection with a proof" is no longer a claim we can make. Our defensible difference is the **open, rerunnable AI-agent time-to-break measurement**, which no vendor found publishes and which mirrors how Euro 7 market surveillance judges protection: by attacking the product. The proof stays in the product as table stakes and as the ISO 26262 tool-qualification argument below.

How OEMs defend today explains why tuning continues. Euro 7 practitioners list SecOC-authenticated CAN, secure boot with code signing, HSM-protected keys and calibrations, and diagnostic write lockdown, with no obfuscation ([Medium summary of IAV talk](https://medium.com/@xeeniq/euro-7-implementing-acts-2025-1706-2025-1707-what-changed-and-the-29-nov-2026-engineering-4eae613b8966)). Bosch ECUs built from about mid-2020 carry HSM-backed secure boot and locked programming, per tool-industry blogs ([Abrites](https://abrites.com/blog/bosch-mg1-md1-ecus-mysteries-myths-and-legends)); in July 2026 several tool vendors announced commercial unlock support for those locked ECUs, which resellers marketed as making bench tuning "mainstream" for independent shops ([obdii365](http://blog.obdii365.com/2026/07/15/obdstar-boot-unlock-bosch-md1-mg1-ecus/); [vxdas](https://www.vxdas.com/blogs/news/obdstar-unlocks-bosch-mg1-md1-encrypted-ecus-bmw-b48-b58-bench-programming-goes-mainstream)). These are unverified reseller claims, but the business pattern is clear: **each protection generation buys a multi-year window and then becomes a paid unlock product**, which is exactly the "time bought" quantity our metric reports. Tuning continues because demand is large and devices cost EUR 10-500 ([CORDIS on DIAS](https://cordis.europa.eu/article/id/443043-hardening-environmental-protection-systems-against-tampering)), because the deterrent that hurts tuners is regulatory and after the fact (several US tuners paid reduced penalties for inability to pay, such as COBB's USD 2.9 million ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/united-states-reaches-agreement-cobb-tuning-products-clean-air-act-violations))), and because no OEM lawsuit against a tuner on IP grounds was found. In e-bikes, Bosch's reference approach since 2019 is detection plus emergency mode, cleared only by a dealer after repeat detection ([Bosch eBike Systems](https://www.bosch-ebike.com/en/service/ebike-tuning)), and it draws consumer complaints about false flags on legitimate repairs ([Consumer Rights Wiki](https://consumerrights.wiki/w/E-Bike_Tuning_Detection:_Bosch_and_Yamaha%E2%80%99s_Anti-Tampering_Systems)).

## Buyers are compliance owners on multi-year cycles, and the AI threat is general rather than automotive

The economic buyer is the OEM cybersecurity manager or automotive CISO who owns the CSMS and signs the cybersecurity case, with procurement and supplier quality as gatekeepers; R155 regulates the OEM, which passes duties to Tier-1s through ISO/SAE 21434 Cybersecurity Interface Agreements ([NHIMG](https://nhimg.org/unece-r155-csms-guide); [Kaspersky ICS CERT](https://ics-cert.kaspersky.com/publications/reports/2024/02/07/cybersecurity-in-the-automotive-industry-ensuring-compliance-with-unece-regulations/)). Our first champion is therefore a product cybersecurity manager at a supplier answering a CIA, and what we sell them is TARA evidence. The cycle is slow: software development starts 3-4 years before production at traditional OEMs, 24-30 months at newer entrants ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/automotive-and-assembly/our-insights/winning-the-automotive-software-development-race)). No public figure for sales-cycle length or for firmware-obfuscation pricing was found; the only price anchors are TARA tools at about EUR 900 to USD 100,000 per year ([CYMETRIS](https://www.cymetris.com/lite/); [vxlabs](https://vxlabs.ai/pricing/)). Small-series OEMs (up to about 10,000 vehicles a year) understand cyber risk but lack certifiable processes, making them a plausible early segment ([PlaxidityX](https://plaxidityx.com/blog/blog-post/automotive-cyber-security-best-practices-for-small-series-oems/)). The precedent for speed is Karamba, which within about a year of its seed had engaged 16 OEMs and Tier-1s and been selected by one of each, selections rather than production revenue ([New Tech Europe](https://www.new-techeurope.com/2017/05/17/karamba-security-closes-12-million-series-b-funding-accelerate-growth-autonomous-vehicle-security-market/)). Exits in this sector go to strategics: Argus to Continental, Cybellum to LG for a reported USD 240 million ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/09/22/lg-is-acquiring-automotive-cybersecurity-startup-cybellum-in-a-240m-deal/)). Emproof's thin public funding after several years, despite already offering a proof, suggests that proof-backed firmware obfuscation alone is a hard venture story.

The formal proof still has concrete safety-certification value, which is presumably why Emproof also offers one. A tool that transforms code after compilation is treated like a compiler under ISO 26262-8 Clause 11; if nothing downstream catches its errors it lands at TCL3, which demands heavy qualification at ASIL C/D, while independent verification of the output can bring it to TCL1 ([Siemens](https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/verificationhorizons/2022/04/13/clearing-the-fog-of-iso-26262-tool-qualification/); [piembsystech](https://piembsystech.com/tool-qualification-tcl-iso-26262/)). An equivalence proof is exactly such a check, so "our proof keeps your protection tool out of a TCL3 project" is a sound line, though no assessor has been asked whether they accept it, and Emproof's TÜV Nord process assessment means we would be matching a competitor here, not leading. The proof says nothing about timing; customers must still re-measure worst-case execution time.

The AI evidence is strong in general and thin for firmware. Google's Big Sleep agent found SQLite CVE-2025-6965 and later five WebKit CVEs ([The Record](https://therecord.media/google-big-sleep-ai-tool-found-bug); [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2025/11/googles-ai-big-sleep-finds-5-new.html)), DARPA's AIxCC finals showed autonomous find-and-patch at machine speed ([Keysight](https://www.keysight.com/blogs/en/tech/nwvs/2025/08/25/aixcc2025-what-it-means-for-device-security)), and automotive vendors say AI is speeding up attackers: Upstream counted 494 incidents in 2025 against 409 in 2024 ([Upstream](https://upstream.io/reports/global-automotive-cybersecurity-report/)), and VicOne calls AI "a double-edged sword" ([VicOne](https://vicone.com/reports/2025-automotive-cybersecurity-report/)). But no source confirms AI-assisted analysis in a real ECU tuning case, most automotive AI evidence concerns LLMs as a new in-car attack surface, and two research searches on LLM analysis of ECU firmware were blocked by tool filters and remain unchecked. The honest line is: "AI agents now find vulnerabilities autonomously; nobody measures how long protected firmware holds against one." Our benchmark fills a measurement gap; it does not rest on documented AI tuning incidents.

## Judges will raise six objections, and four have solid answers

| Objection | Rebuttal | Residual risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Secure boot already solves it." | Secure boot and HSMs give integrity and authenticity; they "don't stop reverse engineering" of a binary, and each locked ECU generation was unlocked commercially within years ([EEJournal](https://www.eejournal.com/article/from-code-chaos-to-cyber-confidence-inside-the-world-of-firmware-obfuscation/); [obdii365](http://blog.obdii365.com/2026/07/15/obdstar-boot-unlock-bosch-md1-mg1-ecus/)). We sit on top of the HSM and protect the code that enforces limits. | Real: for tuning, signed flashing blocks modified code; analysis alone is not tampering. Say "first line is crypto, we are the layer and the measurement." |
| "Obfuscation is security by obscurity." | Agreed it only raises cost; the time-to-break turns that cost into a number a TARA and a market-surveillance test can use. | ECI lists obscurity alone as not state of the art; never pitch it alone. |
| "The law doesn't ask for this." | R155 M7 requires protecting "system data/code"; Annex XIV examples are explicitly "examples", and market surveillance tests real resistance ([EUR-Lex 2025/1706](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32025R1706)). | No authority has accepted obfuscation evidence yet. |
| "Right to repair." | We protect limit and calibration integrity, not diagnostic or reprogramming interfaces; vehicles are outside the EU repair directive; 2018/858 already separates authorised access. | Machinery under Deere-style scrutiny; tamper detection can flag legitimate repairs. |
| "Real-time overhead." | Protect selected functions only; vendor figures put selective protection at 10-20% versus up to 100x for classic obfuscation ([EEJournal](https://www.eejournal.com/article/from-code-chaos-to-cyber-confidence-inside-the-world-of-firmware-obfuscation/)). | No independent benchmark; hot control loops excluded. |
| "Buyers are slow." | Post-compilation protection can ship over the air to fielded devices ([HTGF](https://www.htgf.de/en/htgf-investment-emproof/)); Karamba won selections within a year. | Real: programmes run for years. |
| "Emproof already does this, with a proof." | Yes, and we say so first. Our difference is an open, rerunnable AI-agent time-to-break score that mirrors Euro 7 market-surveillance attack testing; no vendor found publishes one. | Real: Emproof is funded, TÜV-assessed by its own account, and could add a benchmark. |

## How firmware compares with games and professional software

The team's earlier decision ranked the alternatives without external citations in this pass, so this comparison carries moderate confidence ([decision 0002](../decisions/0002-use-case-paid-professional-software.md); [decision 0003](../decisions/0003-use-case-vehicle-and-machine-control-firmware.md)). PC games have the biggest money story (launch-window cracking) but a performance-sensitive engine and entrenched incumbents such as Denuvo and Irdeto. Paid professional software (CAD, simulation, audio) has buyers who already pay for protection and licensing, works offline, and has named leads, but its reported pain skews toward licence operations rather than cracking. Firmware alone has binding law with dates, measured public harm, and an enforcement regime that literally attacks the product, which makes it the best pitch; it also has the slowest buyers, the weakest fit between "obfuscation" and what regulators name, and a competitor, Emproof, that already claims our proof.

## Conclusion

The firmware case changes what the product is. In games and professional software we sell obfuscation that happens to be measurable; in regulated controllers we would sell **measurement that happens to include obfuscation**, because the law and the industry both treat cryptographic integrity as the baseline and judge protection by whether real, market-available attacks succeed. Emproof's proof claim sharpens this further: the proof is now table stakes, and the open benchmark is the only thing we can say first. That reframing is the recommended pitch: "Euro 7 market surveillance will attack your controllers with whatever tools are on the market; we run that attack first, with AI agents, and give you a rerunnable time-to-break number for your TARA and declaration. The protection we add is proven not to change the limit or emission logic, so it never becomes the next Dieselgate." The recommended first segment is second-tier e-bike drive-unit and controller suppliers and small L-category makers (mopeds, motorcycles, speed pedelecs): the duty already exists, the EN 15194 revision lands in Q4 2026 and becomes the de facto purchase spec, a few design wins cover most of the market, and they lack Bosch's in-house capacity. Cars stay the expansion story, machinery last because of right-to-repair exposure. Verdict: **firmware is a strong hackathon pitch and a moderate-to-weak first business; choose it only if we accept that revenue comes later and that the whole differentiation rests on the AI time-to-break benchmark, since Emproof already claims the proof.** One implication for the team's choice between use cases: the proof differentiates us more in paid professional software, where no competitor proof claim was found, than in firmware.

The three cheapest validation steps, in order: (1) **Check whether anyone already publishes an AI time-to-break score for firmware**, in an afternoon: Emproof's site and recent talks, Irdeto, and the escar Europe 2025/2026 agendas; if Emproof or another vendor publishes one, the firmware use case has no first-mover claim left. (2) **Run Luka's spike** on a small self-written motor controller with a 25 km/h limiter: measure an AI agent's time and cost to locate and raise the limit on the plain versus protected build; no measurable gap kills the use case regardless of the market. (3) **Ask five people one question**, product cybersecurity managers at two or three second-tier drive-unit or L-category makers plus one type-approval technical service: "Would a measured time-to-break against an AI agent count as evidence in your TARA or anti-tampering description, alongside secure boot?" Two clear yeses justify the segment; consistent "signed firmware already solves it" answers trigger the revisit clause in decision 0003.
