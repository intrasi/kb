# Insiders, not teardowns, drive machine-software theft

*Synthesised 2026-11-08 from the research notes in `research_notes/Machine software cloning evidence/` (researched 2026-11-07), building on the earlier anti-tampering report (`sources/re-vehicle-firmware-anti-tampering-report-2026-11-07.md`). Business and legal level only; no attack or bypass methods. Not legal advice.*

Yes, competitors have taken machine-control software and shipped it in their own products, and the court-found losses are some of the largest in trade-secret history. But the public record does not support the specific story of "a clone maker pulls John Deere's software off a tractor and runs it on an off-brand machine," and so it does not make "protecting machine makers' know-how against copying" a strong lead use case for us. The two landmark cases are **Sinovel**, where stolen wind-turbine converter control code ran in Sinovel turbines and the court found AMSC's losses **above $550M** ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/court-imposes-maximum-fine-sinovel-wind-group-theft-trade-secrets)), and **Motorola v. Hytera**, a **$764.6M** verdict over radio firmware ([Motorola](https://www.motorolasolutions.com/newsroom/press-releases/motorola-solutions-wins-trade-secret-theft-and-copyright-infringement-lawsu.html)). Both began with insiders carrying source code out of the company, as did Waymo/Uber, GM/Chery and Tesla/Xpeng. Protecting the binary on the machine would not have stopped any of them. No public case was found of Deere, CNH, AGCO, Caterpillar, Komatsu, Liebherr or Bosch control software being copied onto a competitor's machine. German machinery piracy is real (46% of VDMA firms affected, EUR 4.1B in damage, China the origin in 82% of cases), but the survey measures parts, designs and whole machines and does not break out software ([VDMA 2024](https://cdn.wibu.com/fileadmin/wibu_downloads/Product_Brochures/studies_and_surveys/VDMA_Study-Product_Piracy/VDMA_Study_Industrial_Security_and_Product_Piracy_2024.pdf)). The competitive picture is also tighter than earlier notes said. Wibu CodeMeter and Siemens already sell know-how protection to machine builders, and **Emproof already claims "formal proofs such as translation validation" for its embedded firmware obfuscation under ISO 26262** ([Emproof](https://emproof.com/en/solution)). The behaviour proof is therefore not a differentiator; only the open, rerunnable AI-agent time-to-break measurement is. **Verdict: anti-copying belongs in the vision as the second half of "protect the code, not the machine from its owner." It should not be the lead. Anti-tampering (Euro 7, L-category, e-bikes) stays the lead pitch.**

## Hundreds of millions in losses, every big case started with a person

When copied control software actually ships in a competitor's product, court-found harm runs to hundreds of millions of dollars. The table collects the adjudicated or settled cases found for 2010-2026.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| US v. Sinovel (AMSC), 2018 | Wind-turbine converter control source code (PM3000) | AMSC employee downloaded source code to a computer in Austria | Losses found **>$550M**; DOJ also cites >$800M of unpaid contracts, >$1B of equity and ~700 jobs | $1.5M fine plus ~$57.5M restitution ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/court-imposes-maximum-fine-sinovel-wind-group-theft-trade-secrets); [AMSC](https://ir.amsc.com/news-releases/news-release-details/court-imposes-sentence-sinovel-theft-amsc-trade-secrets)) |
| Motorola v. | US v. Du/Qin (GM), 2013 | Hybrid motor-control source code | Employee took >16,000 files for a planned Chery venture | Valued at ~$40M by prosecutors | Prison terms of 1 and 3 years; no proven transfer ([FBI](https://www.fbi.gov/detroit/press-releases/2013/two-convicted-in-conspiracy-to-steal-gm-trade-secrets-sentenced-to-prison)) |

The pattern is unambiguous. **Every large case began with insider access to source code**, and the only case found that started from a purchased product is a small one (Hondata, about $58K). The courts' own findings show the stakes: none of Hytera's products at issue "would function without Motorola's copyrighted source code" ([7th Cir.](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-7th-circuit/116339567.html)). The gap between harm and recovery is also large. AMSC recovered about $57.5M against losses above $550M, which is the classic argument for prevention over litigation. That argument, though, is for preventing the insider leak, not the field teardown. One further ruling cuts against code-level protection as a full answer: Hytera's later swap of the copied code for "functional equivalents" did not cure the misappropriation, because the trade secrets included the **software architecture** ([Motorola](https://www.motorolasolutions.com/newsroom/press-releases/hytera-continues-to-use-stolen-trade-secrets.html)). Architecture know-how travels in engineers' heads, where obfuscation cannot reach.

The brands the question names have no software-copying record. Deere's China litigation is about trademarks, including a RMB 10.55M fine against a Chinese tractor maker for a lookalike logo ([China IP Law Update](https://www.chinaiplawupdate.com/2022/05/chinas-state-administration-for-market-regulation-releases-typical-cases-of-intellectual-property-enforcement-for-2021/)). Deere v. AGCO was a planter-hardware patent case that Deere lost ([DTN](https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/AG/equipment/article/2025/01/28/federal-court-rules-john-deere-case)). Caterpillar and Bobcat fight over hydraulic-control patents ([Equipment Finance News](https://equipmentfinancenews.com/news/construction/bobcat-sues-caterpillar-for-patent-infringement/)). That proves control logic is valuable IP, but it is a patent dispute between two established makers, not a cloning case. China's Supreme People's Court did award RMB 381.63M (about $57M) against a machine-tool maker for an ex-employee's copied technical files, but the report does not say whether control software was involved, and it is again an insider case ([Caixin](https://caixinchinawatch.substack.com/p/in-depth-chinese-machine-maker-hit)). The claim that weaker repair rules would give China "easier access to proprietary software for copying" appears in right-to-repair lobbying, but as an argument, not as evidence of losses ([Farm Equipment](https://www.farm-equipment.com/articles/20002-ongoing-coverage-right-to-repair-impact-on-dealers-deere-other-oems)). Chinese-language court databases and EU customs seizure data were not searched, so the honest statement is "not found in English-language public records," not "does not happen."

## Copying a whole binary needs no understanding; porting the logic does

This is the structural point that decides how much our product can help. A clone maker can misuse a machine maker's software in two ways, and they need different defences.

The first is to **copy the whole binary onto identical or near-identical hardware**. That is what the documented clone-module cases look like: Hondata's counterfeit boards "contained Hondata's proprietary software" ([FBI](https://archives.fbi.gov/archives/losangeles/press-releases/2014/florida-man-charged-in-federal-counterfeit-case-for-trafficking-bogus-automotive-devices-reverse-engineered-in-china)), and Megasquirt lists unlicensed hardware clones running its firmware in at least six European countries ([msextra.com](https://www.msextra.com/clones/), self-published and not adjudicated). This needs no understanding of the code at all, so obfuscation does almost nothing against it. The defence is hardware binding, licensing and signed boot, which is exactly what Wibu CodeMeter already sells to machine builders through CODESYS, binding encrypted, signed code to a dongle or licence file ([Wibu](https://www.wibu.com/us/products/codemeter-integrations/integration-with-codesys.html)). The documented market for this kind of clone is also small and concentrated in modules where the firmware is the product, not in tractors or excavators.

The second is to **port the logic to different hardware**: extract a control algorithm, calibration strategy or motion profile and re-implement it on the clone maker's own controller. This needs understanding, and understanding is what obfuscation of selected functions makes slower and more expensive. It is the scenario where our product actually fits. It is also the scenario with the thinnest public evidence. VDMA's 2016 study found reverse engineering to be the most common source of counterfeits (69%), ahead of know-how lost through former staff (32%) ([VDMA 2016](https://cdn.wibu.com/fileadmin/wibu_downloads/Product_Brochures/studies_and_surveys/VDMA_Study-Product_Piracy/VDMA_Study_Product_Piracy_2016_final.pdf)). But that figure covers all product piracy, mostly mechanical, and is ten years old. The 2024 survey dropped the question entirely. It reports that "digital services" plagiarism rose about fivefold to 11% of cases, that entire machines are copied in about 27%, and that professional plagiarists (42%) and state-owned enterprises (18%) are rising, with samples of only 27-47 firms per detail question ([VDMA 2024](https://cdn.wibu.com/fileadmin/wibu_downloads/Product_Brochures/studies_and_surveys/VDMA_Study-Product_Piracy/VDMA_Study_Industrial_Security_and_Product_Piracy_2024.pdf)). VDMA also notes that getting around trade-secret protection "usually requires a great deal of effort." That supports the time-to-break framing: protection is judged by how long it holds against capable, well-funded copiers.

So our product addresses the slice of the copying problem with the least documentation (outsiders porting logic from a fielded device). It does not address the slice with the big losses (insiders) or the slice with the frequent cases (whole-binary clones, already covered by licensing and hardware binding). A pitch that cites Sinovel's $550M as the problem we solve would be challenged, rightly, by any judge who reads the DOJ release.

## The money is in license-gated features, which is really an anti-tampering story

Machine makers are moving value into software, and this is where the strongest "why it matters" argument lies. Deere expects **$2-3B of its planned ~$24B growth to 2030** to come from "tech utilization via SaaS and additional lifecycle sales". It sells harvest automation and ExactShot on annual licences and See & Spray per acre (over 5 million acres in 2025), and it built global licence-management infrastructure in 2025 ([Deere Investor Day transcript](https://s22.q4cdn.com/253594569/files/doc_downloads/2025/12/09/John-Deere-NYSE-Investor-Day-Transcript.pdf)). Acquisition prices show what precision-ag software IP is worth: CNH paid $2.1B for Raven ([DTN](https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/news/article/2021/08/01/new-tech-giant)) and AGCO about $2.0B for 85% of Trimble's ag business ([AGCO](https://investors.agcocorp.com/news-releases/news-release-details/agco-acquire-trimble-ag-assets-and-technologies-through-joint)). The revenue is still small and slipping, though. Deere has moved its "10% recurring revenue" goal to "beyond 2030" ([Deere transcript](https://s22.q4cdn.com/253594569/files/doc_downloads/2025/12/09/John-Deere-NYSE-Investor-Day-Transcript.pdf)). AGCO pushed its $2B PTx target to 2029 and took a $350M impairment ([Precision Farming Dealer](https://www.precisionfarmingdealer.com/articles/6549-agco-shares-update-on-precision-technology-strategy-and-growth-outlook)). The pain is mostly in the future.

The threat to those features is mainly **unauthorised unlocking by the owner**, not a competitor copying them. Once a paid feature sits on hardware the customer owns, grey-market unlocking follows; BMW's heated-seat subscription drew one-time unlock offers for under about $50 ([Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/bmw-wants-to-charge-for-heated-seats-these-grey-market-hackers-will-fix-that/)). No business-level record of unlocking Deere, CNH or AGCO licensed features was found, and that search was partly blocked, so it remains open. Unlocking is a licence-integrity problem, closer to the anti-tampering use case than to know-how protection. It is also the politically most dangerous ground (see the next section). In agriculture, "stop the farmer enabling a feature on his own tractor" sits right next to the practices the FTC just settled with Deere.

## The law protects code from copiers, not machines from their owners

The legal picture fixes what we can sell. In the EU, a lawful user may observe, study and test a program to learn "the ideas and principles which underlie" it (Software Directive Art 5(3)). Decompiling for interoperability is permitted under strict conditions (Art 6(1)), and Art 8 makes contract terms that override these rights null and void ([EUR-Lex 2009/24](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32009L0024)). The CJEU added decompilation for error correction in *Top System* ([EUR-Lex C-13/20](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=celex:62020CJ0013)). Under the Trade Secrets Directive, teardown of a lawfully held product is a lawful way to acquire a secret unless validly contracted away (Art 3(1)(b), Recital 16) ([EUR-Lex 2016/943](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32016L0943)). That has a sharp consequence. **A competitor who buys a machine and studies its software is, in principle, acting lawfully in the EU.** What the law forbids is the next step: using decompiled information to build "a substantially similar program" (Art 6(2)(c)), and selling tools whose sole purpose is to circumvent protection (Art 7(1)(c)). Obfuscation helps the machine maker on two fronts. It raises the cost of the lawful-but-harmful study, and it counts as evidence of the "reasonable steps" that trade-secret status requires (TSD Art 2(1)(c)). But the machine maker's case against a cloner rests mostly on copyright, not trade secret.

The US is stricter by default but has a large carve-out for exactly these machines. DMCA §1201 bans circumventing access controls, which our protection would be, and separately bans trafficking in circumvention tools ([17 U.S.C. §1201](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/1201)). Since 2015, however, the Copyright Office has exempted circumvention for diagnosis, repair and lawful modification of vehicles, including mechanized agricultural vehicles. Since 2018 that covers third parties acting for the owner, and since 2024 it adds owner access to operational and telematics data ([37 CFR 201.40](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/37/201.40); [Register's Recommendation 2024](https://www.copyright.gov/1201/2024/2024_Section_1201_Registers_Recommendation.pdf)). The exemption explicitly gives no shelter under EPA or DOT rules. A tenth cycle is running now, and renewed exemptions would run from October 2027 to October 2030 ([Copyright Office](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USLOCCOPYRIGHT/bulletins/41a9e0d)).

Repair law then constrains what the protected software may do. Under the **FTC v. Deere** stipulated order of 8 July 2026, Deere must give farmers and independent repairers dealer-equivalent resources for ten years: fault codes, ECU reprogramming, pairing new parts and restarting a machine after an emissions-related shutdown. A parallel private class settlement carries a $99M fund ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/07/ftc-states-secure-settlement-deere-company-advancing-farmers-right-repair); [Paul Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/ftc-settles-right-to-repair-monopolization-case-against-deere)). The **EU Data Act**, applicable since 12 September 2025, lets data holders protect trade secrets through agreed confidentiality measures and bars users from using shared data to build competing products. But it allows outright refusal only where serious economic damage is "highly likely," and data cannot be withheld merely because it is a trade secret ([EUR-Lex 2023/2854](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/2854/oj/eng)). The Right to Repair Directive already names "software techniques that impede the repair" as prohibited unless justified by legitimate factors including IP protection. It covers appliances and e-bike batteries today, not vehicles or tractors, but the Commission can extend it ([EUR-Lex 2024/1799](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=CELEX:32024L1799)).

**Newag is the template for what goes wrong.** Polish Impuls trains were found to lock themselves after standing in independent repair yards (identified by GPS), after non-approved parts were fitted, or at a mileage threshold. The logic was found in 24 of 30 trains examined. Operators reportedly paid about EUR 23,000 per train to unlock them. CERT Polska endorsed the researchers' findings, the Sejm held three hearings, and antitrust and criminal probes followed. Newag's suits against the researchers kept the story alive for three years ([The Rail Agenda](https://www.therailagenda.com/p/why-newag-trains-stopped-working); [iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/News/112008/polish-train-maker-is-suing-the-hackers-who-exposed-its-anti-repair-tricks)). Obfuscation is what would have made that logic harder to find. If a customer hides anti-repair logic behind our protection, we become part of the next Newag story. The defensible framing follows from all of this: **"we protect the code, not the machine from its owner."** We protect algorithms and calibrations against copying and certified limits against tampering. Every service, diagnostic, data and reprogramming function the law requires stays reachable through the maker's documented channel. Contracts ban geofenced lockouts, parts-pairing traps and hidden defeat logic. Regulators or certifiers can get the unprotected build or an audit. Here the behaviour proof earns its place as a trust feature, even though it does not differentiate us: it shows that protection added no hidden function. We should never market the product as stopping "reverse engineering" in general (much of that is a non-waivable right in the EU), and never as legal pressure on researchers.

## Incumbents already sell know-how protection, and Emproof already claims the proof

Machine builders already buy this category, which is both good news (a budget exists) and bad news (incumbents hold it). Wibu CodeMeter protects "machine sequences, process parameters, control algorithms, motion profiles" inside the CODESYS platform and in late 2025 shipped a microcontroller library of a few kilobytes ([Wibu](https://www.wibu.com/us/products/codemeter-integrations/integration-with-codesys.html); [Wibu SPS 2025](https://www.wibu.com/press-releases/press-release-details/detail/wibu-systems-at-sps-2025-codemeter-powers-licensing-and-security-from-microcontrollers-to-enterprise-systems.html)). Wibu is also a VDMA partner and distributes the VDMA piracy studies, so the main survey evidence comes through a vendor channel. Siemens sells know-how protection in TIA Portal. Irdeto Cloakware offers obfuscation and anti-tamper for vehicles and industrial IoT ([Irdeto/ARC](https://go.irdeto.com/arc-white-paper-irdeto-enables-more-secure-deployments-of-industrialiot-devices/)).

**Emproof** is the direct competitor, and one research note needs correcting. The note on software value concluded that no incumbent offers a behaviour proof. That is wrong. Emproof's Nyx does binary-level anti-reverse-engineering for Arm, x86 and RISC-V, explicitly targets industrial IoT "control and automation software" ([Emproof IIoT](https://www.emproof.com/sectors/industrial-iot/)), and states "we use formal proofs such as translation validation to ensure the correctness of our translations" under ISO 26262 ([Emproof](https://emproof.com/en/solution)). Its service description cites ASIL D for the Enterprise edition, with a process assessed by TÜV Nord ([Emproof service description](https://emproof.com/en/service-description)). These are vendor claims, not an independent audit, but the business conclusion stands: obfuscation of selected functions plus an equivalence proof is already on the market. **The only differentiator left is the open, rerunnable AI-agent time-to-break measurement**, which no vendor was found to publish. That gap is narrower in the copying use case than in anti-tampering. Euro 7 market surveillance explicitly judges protection by reproducing attacks with market-available tools ([EUR-Lex 2025/1706](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32025R1706)), so a time-to-break number maps onto a legal test. In know-how protection, no regulator or standard asks for one, and the buyer has to be persuaded that the number means something.

## Conclusion

The research reframes what "know-how protection" can honestly promise. The dramatic losses in this field are failures of **people and source-code access**. The frequent clone cases are **whole-binary copies** that licensing and hardware binding already handle. Our product's real contribution sits in between: raising the cost of understanding and porting selected algorithms from a fielded device, and giving the maker evidence of "reasonable steps." That is a valid and probably growing need. Professional plagiarists and state-owned firms are rising in VDMA's data, and AI agents plausibly cut the cost of understanding code. But we cannot point to it as a documented, quantified loss, and we would be selling it against Wibu, Siemens, Irdeto and an Emproof that already claims our proof. The useful insight is that the two use cases share one product and one sentence. **"Protect the code, not the machine from its owner"** covers anti-tampering (keep certified limits intact) and anti-copying (keep algorithms from being ported). The open time-to-break score is the common evidence layer. Anti-tampering carries the legal deadline and the measured harm, so it leads.

**Verdict: part of the vision, not the lead, and not dropped.** Keep anti-tampering as the lead use case. Mention know-how protection as the second market for the same engine, aimed at mid-size machine builders and CODESYS/PLC application vendors as an add-on to licensing, not at Deere-scale OEMs with in-house security teams and right-to-repair exposure. Never pitch Sinovel or Hytera as "the problem we solve"; cite them only as evidence that control software is worth hundreds of millions to a copier. The cheapest validation steps, in order:

1. **One afternoon on the evidence gap.** Search Chinese-language court records (China Judgments Online) and EU customs seizure reports for copied machine control software, and check Wibu, Emproof and Irdeto for any published time-to-break figure. A documented porting case would strengthen this use case; a competitor's published benchmark would remove our last differentiator in both use cases.
2. **Five conversations, one question.** Ask product-security or engineering leads at three or four mid-size machine builders or CODESYS application vendors, plus one VDMA product-protection contact: "When a competitor copied your control logic, did it come through people, a whole-device copy, or someone working out the code from a machine you sold?" If the answer is consistently "people," the use case stays vision only. If "working out the code" comes up twice, it earns a second slot in the pitch.
3. **Reuse Luka's spike with a porting task.** On the same small self-written controller, measure the time and cost for an AI agent to reproduce the behaviour of one selected function on the plain build versus the protected build. Do this alongside the limiter-raising task from the anti-tampering spike, so one experiment produces evidence for both use cases.
